§ 01 — DEFINITION

What Is an Artificial Satellite?

The foundational concept behind every spacecraft in orbit today.

An artificial satellite is a human-made object placed into orbit around a celestial body — most commonly Earth. Unlike natural satellites such as the Moon, artificial satellites are engineered spacecraft carrying sensors, transmitters, and onboard systems that serve specific missions. Since Sputnik I in 1957, thousands of satellites have been launched, transforming global communication, navigation, weather forecasting, and scientific research.

A satellite stays in orbit because it is falling toward Earth — but moving sideways fast enough to keep missing it.

§ 02 — HISTORY

From Newton to New Space

The key milestones that shaped satellite technology over seven decades.

1687
Newton's Cannonball Thought Experiment
Isaac Newton demonstrated mathematically that if a projectile were launched horizontally with enough speed from a high mountain, it would orbit Earth rather than fall back. This was the first theoretical basis for artificial satellites — nearly 270 years before it was physically achieved.
4 Oct 1957
Sputnik I — World's First Artificial Satellite
The Soviet Union launched Sputnik I, a polished 83.6 kg sphere carrying only two radio transmitters. It orbited Earth every 96 minutes and transmitted radio pulses at 20 MHz. Its launch triggered the Space Race and proved intercontinental delivery of payloads was feasible.
31 Jan 1958
Explorer I — U.S. Enters the Space Age
America's response to Sputnik. Explorer I discovered the Van Allen radiation belts — zones of charged particles trapped by Earth's magnetic field. It remained in orbit until 1970, proving that scientific instruments on satellites could make fundamental discoveries.
1958
NASA Founded
The U.S. government established NASA to coordinate civilian space exploration. NASA rapidly evolved satellite technology — launching communication, weather, and scientific satellites throughout the 1960s and beyond.
1962
Telstar 1 — First Live TV Across the Atlantic
The first active communication satellite, Telstar 1 relayed the first live transatlantic television signal. This marked the dawn of satellite-based global communication — the direct ancestor of today's TV broadcast and internet satellites.
1964
Syncom 3 — First Geostationary Satellite
Syncom 3 was positioned at 35,786 km above the equator, orbiting at exactly the same rate Earth rotates. From the ground, it appeared stationary in the sky — making it ideal for continuous TV and communication relay without tracking dishes.
1970s–1990s
GPS, Remote Sensing & the Modern Satellite Era
GPS navigation satellites, Landsat Earth observation, and weather satellites like GOES transformed how humanity interacts with the planet. By the 1990s, satellites were embedded into defense, agriculture, aviation, and telecommunications infrastructure worldwide.
§ 03 — ORBITAL MECHANICS

Orbits & Satellite Dynamics

Where a satellite orbits determines everything — its latency, coverage area, power needs, and lifespan.

Orbital mechanics follow Kepler's Laws: satellites in lower orbits move faster and complete orbits more quickly. The altitude chosen is a critical engineering decision — it governs signal delay, atmospheric drag, radiation exposure, and the size of ground footprint covered with each pass.

Orbit Altitude Period Latency Use Cases
LEO Low Earth Orbit 200 – 2,000 km 90 – 120 min 20 – 40 ms ISS, Starlink, imaging, spy satellites
MEO Medium Earth Orbit 2,000 – 35,786 km 2 – 24 hrs 77 – 160 ms GPS, GLONASS, Galileo navigation
GEO Geostationary Orbit 35,786 km 24 hrs (sync) 600 ms TV broadcast, weather, fixed comms
HEO — Highly Elliptical Variable 12+ hrs Variable Polar coverage, Molniya orbits
SSO — Sun-Synchronous 600 – 800 km ~98 min ~30 ms Earth observation, consistent lighting

Orbital perturbations — small disturbances from Earth's non-uniform gravity, atmospheric drag, solar radiation pressure, and the Moon's gravity — cause orbits to shift over time. Satellites carry onboard propulsion to make small corrections called station-keeping manoeuvres. When fuel runs out, the satellite is decommissioned.

Satellite Subsystems

🏗️
Structural Subsystem
The Frame
Provides the mechanical skeleton that holds all components together and withstands the violent vibrations of launch. Built from lightweight materials like aluminium alloys, carbon fibre, and titanium to minimise mass while maintaining rigidity.
⚡
Power Subsystem (EPS)
Solar Panels + Batteries
Solar panels convert sunlight into electricity. Batteries store power for periods when the satellite passes through Earth's shadow (eclipses). Power management is critical — all other subsystems depend on it. Most satellites use GaAs solar cells for efficiency in space.
🌡️
Thermal Control (TCS)
Managing Extremes
Space swings from +150°C in direct sunlight to −150°C in shadow — within the same orbit. The TCS uses passive methods (surface coatings, insulation blankets) and active methods (heaters, heat pipes) to keep all components within their operating temperature range.
🧭
ADCS
Attitude Control
Determines and controls satellite orientation. Uses sensors (star trackers, gyroscopes, magnetometers) to know which way the satellite is pointing, and actuators (reaction wheels, thrusters) to adjust it. Critical for keeping antennas toward Earth and solar panels toward the Sun.
📡
Communications
The Payload Link
Handles transmission and reception of signals between satellite and ground stations. Uses frequency bands: L, S, C, X, Ku, Ka — higher frequencies carry more data but are more affected by rain and atmospheric absorption. Directional antennas maximise gain.
🔧
TT&C
Telemetry, Tracking & Command
The lifeline between ground operators and the satellite. Telemetry sends housekeeping data (temperatures, voltages, status) to Earth. Tracking determines the satellite's exact position. Command uplinks instructions from ground control to the satellite's onboard computer.
§ 05 — CATEGORIES

Types of Satellites

Satellites are classified by their primary mission — each category reshaping a different sector of human activity.

📺
Category 01
Communication Satellites
Act as radio relay stations in orbit. They receive signals from one ground station, amplify them, and retransmit to another location — enabling intercontinental phone calls, TV broadcasts, and internet connectivity. GEO is preferred for broadcast due to fixed sky position.
🗺️
Category 02
Navigation Satellites (GNSS)
GPS (USA), GLONASS (Russia), Galileo (EU), and BeiDou (China) form global constellations at MEO. Each satellite broadcasts precise timing signals; receivers calculate position by measuring time differences from at least 4 satellites — accurate to within centimetres.
🌍
Category 03
Earth Observation / Remote Sensing
Carry cameras and sensors to observe Earth's surface. Used for agriculture monitoring, deforestation tracking, disaster response, urban planning, and military reconnaissance. ESA's Sentinel series and NASA's Landsat are key examples — imaging in visible, infrared, and radar wavelengths.
🌦️
Category 04
Weather Satellites
Monitor atmospheric conditions, cloud patterns, storms, and sea surface temperatures. GEO weather satellites (like GOES) provide continuous regional images; LEO satellites provide full global coverage. Their data feeds into every weather forecast on Earth.
🔭
Category 05
Scientific Satellites
Dedicated to scientific research — observing the universe without atmospheric distortion (Hubble Space Telescope), studying Earth's magnetic field, measuring ozone levels, or analysing cosmic radiation. These expand fundamental human knowledge beyond what ground-based instruments can achieve.
🌐
Category 06
Internet Mega-Constellations
SpaceX Starlink, Amazon Kuiper, and OneWeb deploy hundreds to thousands of LEO satellites to deliver broadband internet globally — including to remote regions with no terrestrial infrastructure. Starlink alone has over 6,000 satellites as of 2024, the largest constellation ever.
§ 06 — CHALLENGES

Problems Facing Satellite Technology

The space environment and scale of modern satellite operations present serious challenges that must be solved.

  • 🗑️
    Space Debris
    Over 27,000 pieces of trackable debris orbit Earth — defunct satellites, rocket stages, and fragments from collisions. At orbital speeds (~7.9 km/s), even a 1 cm fragment carries the kinetic energy of a grenade. The Kessler Syndrome describes a cascade where debris generates more debris, eventually making some orbits unusable.
  • 🚦
    Orbital Congestion
    Popular orbital slots — especially GEO and LEO — are becoming crowded. Mega-constellations raise the risk of conjunctions (close approaches) and collisions. Coordination through the ITU (International Telecommunication Union) and new collision-avoidance systems are essential.
  • 💸
    High Launch Costs
    Despite reusable rockets (SpaceX Falcon 9), launching a satellite remains expensive — $2,500–$10,000 per kilogram to LEO. Cost limits access for smaller nations and organisations, though smallsats and rideshare programmes are reducing this barrier significantly.
  • ☢️
    Radiation Environment
    Space is flooded with cosmic rays, solar energetic particles, and trapped radiation in the Van Allen belts. Radiation degrades solar cells, corrupts memory, and can permanently damage electronics. Satellites require radiation-hardened components — more expensive and less powerful than commercial equivalents.
  • 🔒
    Cybersecurity Threats
    Satellites are increasingly targeted by electronic warfare — jamming (disrupting the signal), spoofing (sending false signals), and cyberattacks on ground station software. A successful attack on a navigation or communications satellite can have cascading effects across critical infrastructure on Earth.
  • 🌑
    Astronomical Light Pollution
    Large LEO constellations create visible streaks in astronomical observations, interfering with telescope imaging. The astronomical community and satellite operators are negotiating standards for satellite brightness, darker coatings, and adjusted orbital parameters to mitigate the impact.
§ 07 — THE FUTURE

Where Satellites Are Heading

The next decade will see satellite technology evolve faster than any previous era.

🛰️
Mega-Constellations
Thousands of LEO satellites delivering global broadband — Starlink, Kuiper, and OneWeb racing to connect every corner of Earth.
🔬
CubeSats & SmallSats
Miniaturised satellites the size of a shoebox democratise space access for universities, startups, and developing nations.
🔗
Quantum Satellite Comms
Quantum key distribution via satellite promises theoretically unbreakable encryption — China's Micius satellite has already demonstrated this at intercontinental distances.
🤖
On-Orbit Servicing
Robotic spacecraft that can refuel, repair, and reposition satellites extend their operational lives — reducing debris and launch costs dramatically.
🌙
Lunar & Deep Space
NASA's Artemis programme and commercial lunar missions will require new satellite infrastructure around the Moon for navigation and communications.
♻️
Active Debris Removal
ESA's ClearSpace-1 mission (2026) will attempt to capture and deorbit a defunct satellite — the first active debris removal mission in history.

Satellites are not just infrastructure — they are the nervous system of our civilisation, silently enabling every GPS route, weather alert, and video call across the planet.
