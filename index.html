§ 02 — WHAT I LEARNED

Core Topics in Data Communications

A structured overview of the key concepts, models, and technologies covered throughout the course.

📡
TOPIC — 01
The OSI Model
The 7-layer Open Systems Interconnection model provides a conceptual framework for how data travels from one device to another — Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, Session, Presentation, and Application layers each play a distinct role.
🔗
TOPIC — 02
TCP/IP Protocol Suite
The backbone of the internet. I learned how Transmission Control Protocol ensures reliable, ordered delivery of data packets, while IP handles addressing and routing across interconnected networks worldwide.
〰️
TOPIC — 03
Signals & Modulation
Understanding analog vs digital signals, and how modulation techniques (AM, FM, PM, QAM) encode digital data onto carrier waves for transmission across various media — the bridge between physical reality and binary data.
🛡️
TOPIC — 04
Error Detection & Correction
Data rarely travels perfectly. I studied parity bits, checksums, CRC (Cyclic Redundancy Check), and Hamming codes — techniques that detect and correct errors introduced during transmission over noisy channels.
🔀
TOPIC — 05
Multiplexing Techniques
TDM (Time-Division), FDM (Frequency-Division), and WDM (Wavelength-Division) multiplexing allow multiple signals to share a single channel simultaneously — crucial for maximising bandwidth efficiency in modern networks.
📶
TOPIC — 06
Wireless Communication
From Wi-Fi to cellular networks, I explored radio frequency propagation, CSMA/CA protocols, IEEE 802.11 standards, and the evolution from 1G to 5G — understanding the engineering behind cutting-edge wireless systems.
🔐
TOPIC — 07
Data Security & Encryption
Securing data in transit through symmetric (AES, DES) and asymmetric (RSA) encryption, TLS/SSL handshakes, and public key infrastructure — essential knowledge for building trustworthy communication systems.
🌐
TOPIC — 08
Network Topologies & Media
Physical and logical topologies (bus, ring, star, mesh), transmission media (twisted pair, coaxial, fiber optic, wireless), and the trade-offs between cost, speed, reliability, and distance in network design.
🛰️
TOPIC — 09
Satellite Systems
Artificial satellites orbiting Earth enable global communication, navigation (GPS), weather forecasting, and earth observation. Understanding orbital mechanics, satellite subsystems, and space-based networks reveals how modern connectivity spans the planet.
§ 02b — SATELLITE COMMUNICATIONS

Satellites: Connecting the Global Network

From geostationary TV broadcast to mega-constellations delivering broadband—satellite systems are a critical layer of modern data communications infrastructure.

📡
ORBIT TYPE 01
Low Earth Orbit (LEO)
200–2,000 km altitude. Satellites orbit every 90–120 minutes with low signal latency (20–40 ms). Used for imaging, Starlink internet, and spy satellites. Fast but requires constellation of satellites for continuous coverage.
🌍
ORBIT TYPE 02
Geostationary Orbit (GEO)
35,786 km altitude. Orbits in 24 hours — appears fixed in the sky from Earth. Perfect for TV broadcast and weather monitoring, but higher latency (600 ms) and fewer satellites needed. The backbone of global television.
🗺️
ORBIT TYPE 03
Medium Earth Orbit (MEO)
2,000–35,786 km altitude. Home to GPS, GLONASS, and Galileo navigation systems. Orbits every 2–24 hours with moderate latency (77–160 ms). The sweet spot for global navigation coverage with manageable constellation size.
Why Satellites Matter in Data Communications:
Satellite systems carry roughly 80% of global data traffic. They enable long-distance communication by relaying signals between ground stations that would otherwise be too far apart. Unlike terrestrial networks, satellites can instantly provide coverage to remote areas—a critical advantage for emergency response, maritime operations, and connecting developing nations to the global internet.
⚡
Key Challenge
Signal Latency
GEO satellites introduce ~600ms delay (signal travels 36,000 km up and down). For real-time applications like video calls, this is noticeable. LEO constellations solve this with lower altitude but require more satellites and handoff complexity.
🗑️
Growing Problem
Space Debris
Over 27,000 trackable objects orbit Earth. At 7.9 km/s, even 1 cm fragments carry grenade-level kinetic energy. The Kessler Syndrome warns that collisions could cascade into unusability of key orbits—a critical infrastructure risk.
🌐
Revolution
Mega-Constellations
SpaceX Starlink (6,000+ satellites), Amazon Kuiper, and OneWeb are deploying thousands of LEO satellites to deliver global broadband. This represents the largest infrastructure buildout since the internet itself—reshaping global connectivity in real-time.

§ 03 — HISTORICAL CONTEXT

The Telegraph: Where It All Began

The electric telegraph was the world's first practical data communication system — a revolutionary technology that compressed distance and time.

My Understanding of the Telegraph

The electric telegraph, invented by Samuel Morse and Alfred Vail in the 1830s–40s, was the world's first practical electrical communication system. It is, in essence, the great-great-grandparent of every digital communication system we use today.

What struck me most is how elegantly simple it is: a battery, a wire, and an electromagnet. The sender presses a key to complete or break an electrical circuit, sending pulses of current down the wire. The receiver's electromagnet clicks in response — long or short. These clicks are Morse code.

The fundamental principles it introduced that still govern data comm:

  • The concept of a channel — a dedicated medium for signal transmission between two points, which maps directly to our modern understanding of communication channels.
  • Encoding — converting a message (letters, words) into a transmittable signal format (dots and dashes / electrical pulses). This is exactly what modems and codecs do today.
  • Binary signalling — the telegraph only had two states: circuit open or closed, current or no current. This is the direct precursor to binary 0s and 1s in digital communication.
  • Ground return — using the Earth as the return path for current. This early insight about circuits is foundational to understanding electrical signal paths.
  • Long-distance relay stations — signals weaken over distance (attenuation). Telegraph operators repeated signals at intervals — exactly what modern network repeaters and amplifiers do.
